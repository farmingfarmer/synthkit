Synthetic cohort for the UM tool evaluation

What the smallest useful cohort actually is, which of his rule types it can exercise, and the three pieces of work that would add more without needing more real patients.

The request was the smallest synthetic cohort that still contains the patterns that matter. The honest answer is that the trade runs the other way: 800 patients is already below what his most important rules need, so there is no smaller cohort that preserves them. What follows is why, and what we can do about it at the patient count we have.

1Why a smaller cohort buys nothing

It is reasonable to assume a smaller release is a safer release. Here it is not, because of when the protection is applied.

Our privacy rule operates while the model learns from the real data — no pattern is carried forward unless at least ten different patients share it. By the time any synthetic file is written, that screening has already happened. Writing fewer synthetic patients removes none of the real patients from the learning step, so it gives away nothing less.

It does cost evidence. Several of his trigger types need the full 800 patients' worth of support, and one sits exactly on that line with no margin.

Measured, not assumed

Two attackers were run against the output, including a positive control — the same attack pointed at a deliberately leaky generator that simply republishes real records. The control is caught every time at a perfect score; the real output reads at close to chance. A test that cannot fail proves nothing, which is why the control is run alongside.

2What 800 patients can and cannot exercise

His rules fall into kinds, and the kinds differ enormously in how much evidence they need. Figures below come from a controlled benchmark where each pattern was planted at known strength and we measured how many patients were needed before it came through reliably.

Two caveats on the patient counts. They are the largest size we swept, so they are a floor rather than a proven sufficiency. And the benchmark averaged four visits per patient where this cohort averages around sixty-five — more records per patient should help, but we have not measured that, so treat the figures as bounds we have no evidence are too low.

What this means in one line: the cohort is a good functional test of his tool — does it ingest our data, parse the fields, fire the right rules, handle the edge cases — and a weak test of his multi-factor clinical logic. Saying so up front is better than a clean result implying we validated something we did not.

3Why the combination rules fail, precisely

This is worth understanding because it is not simply "not enough data", and the distinction decides which work is worth doing.

Across all 800 patients we can detect a faint trace of these combinations. Reproducing them is a different bar. The patients who sit in all three groups at once — elderly, renally impaired, on that drug — are a small handful, and our rule only reproduces a pattern when ten patients share it. So the specific group carrying the combination falls below the floor even though the faint signal across the whole cohort is visible. We can see the shadow; we are not permitted to publish what casts it.

On top of that, the protection itself works by disregarding patterns that only a few records support — that is exactly what makes the output survive the attack testing above. The mechanism that keeps the data safe is the same one that removes thin combinations. A genuine trade, not something to tune away.

And in our own controlled testing, two-factor combinations come through reliably while three-factor ones mostly do not, even with ample data. So more patients is a necessary first step and not a guaranteed fix. We would not want to promise otherwise.

4Three pieces of work that add patterns at the same 800 patients

These are the constructive options. None needs a wider extract.

A. Publish more of the vocabulary we are already allowed to publish — smallest, already underway

Our generator caps how many distinct codes it models, for memory reasons rather than privacy ones. On an 800-patient sample, one condition column held about 4,700 codes that already cleared the privacy floor, and only sixty were being modelled. The cap has since been raised, and a sweep now running tests raising it further. The first result shows every measured pattern family improving; it is a single run so far and two more are in progress before we would report it as settled.

This is the cheapest work available, because the discarded codes were already cleared for release — nothing about the privacy position changes.

B. A transition model for within-stay trends — the missing rule family

His trend rules currently cannot be tested at all. The approach that would address it: fit a model on the real patients' courses that learns what tends to happen next given a patient's current state, then use it to extend each synthetic patient forward.

The reason this is more promising than it first appears: learning transitions pools evidence across the whole cohort, where learning whole trajectories would need ten patients sharing a near-identical course. "Haemoglobin tends to fall after this diagnosis" can be fitted from every patient who had it.

Two things decide whether it works, and both are cheap to test before committing to a build. First, the model must reproduce the variability of change and not just the average, or every synthetic patient's labs glide smoothly and nobody ever drops two points in a day — the very rule we are trying to support. Second, a patient's course is far more identifying than a single visit, so the attack battery has to be re-run against it. Our recommendation is a short feasibility spike that answers both with numbers rather than a full build.

A prior question to settle first, and it is quick: our extract holds one row per visit. If serial measurements within a single stay were collapsed at extraction, the trend is not in our source data at all, and no model can synthesise what the source does not contain.

C. Publish the combination patterns as screened summaries

A route to the two-factor family that keeps the current privacy position: identify the combinations that matter, then publish them as aggregate summaries screened by the same ten-patient rule, which the generator samples from. This imports the pattern without releasing anything record-level. It is the most speculative of the three and the one we would scope last.

5What only a wider extract fixes

More patients — in the region of three thousand or more — would lift the small groups above the privacy floor and bring the two- and three-factor rules into range. More rows from the same patients does nothing, because the floor counts people. Nor does generating a larger synthetic file: that reproduces what the model already learned from the 800, and more rows would imply more evidence than exists.

6Recommendation

Hand over the full 800-patient cohort for the functional evaluation now, with the rule-kind table above stated plainly so his team knows which of their logic is being exercised and which is not.

Then, in order of cost: finish the vocabulary work already running (A), run the feasibility spike on within-stay trends (B), and treat the combination summaries (C) as a later decision informed by B's result. Separately, if a wider pull is possible, that is the single change that moves the most.

One measurement note for completeness. While preparing these figures we found a defect in our own measurement code: patient and visit identifiers were being counted as if they were clinical factors, which inflated one of the combination figures. That measurement is being re-run. We expect it to confirm the position above rather than change it, and will say so either way before anything is handed over.

Every figure here comes from a run that can be repeated, and the measurements that do not flatter us are included deliberately.
